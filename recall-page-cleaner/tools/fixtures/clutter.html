How Tides Work

By Ada Example · 1 October 2026

Share on XFacebookEmail

The tides are the regular rise and fall of the sea, caused mostly by the Moon's gravity pulling on the oceans, and to a lesser extent by the Sun. Because the Earth rotates beneath these bulges of water, most coasts see two high tides and two low tides a day, roughly twelve hours and twenty-five minutes apart.

Advertisement

The difference between high and low water, the tidal range, varies enormously from place to place. In the open ocean it may be less than a metre, while in funnel-shaped bays, such as the Bay of Fundy, it can exceed fifteen metres, because the shape of the coast amplifies the incoming wave.

Spring tides, with the largest range, happen near new and full moon, when the Sun and Moon line up and their pulls add together. Neap tides, with the smallest range, come at the quarter moons, when the two pulls are at right angles and partly cancel out.

Tide tables are computed by harmonic analysis: the observed water level at a port is broken down into dozens of regular components, each tied to a motion of the Earth, Moon or Sun, and these are then added back together to predict the water level years ahead.
